# Chapter 3

By midnight the city was still running, but it had begun to show seams.

Public screens carried soft advisories about transient orientation delays. Delivery routes rerouted around intersections where drivers had frozen at green lights with tears on their faces. A surgeon in Lavapies finished a procedure while describing snow that was not there. Two police officers walked into the lobby of a district court and failed to recognize the building they worked in. Above it all, the ad mesh kept trying to sell calm with discount hydration plans and upgrade financing. Nexora's official account posted reassurance every thirty minutes in the polished grammar of expensive certainty.

Vigil remains safe. Temporary drift events are rare. A precision update will improve user comfort.

Comfort.

I sat with Ines in an auxiliary maintenance room beneath Nexora's distribution hub, where the air smelled of coolant and dust sealed for too many years behind clean white walls. Fiber trunks ran overhead like exposed roots under frosted shielding. She had ghosted us through three permissions layers using credentials that would burn the instant the central system noticed where we were. On my tablet the restoration package waited in a locked queue beside the internal studies, the convergence projections, and the memos that proved executive knowledge.

"Once this goes," she said, "you don't get to call it a warning shot."

"I know."

"People are going to say you caused the collapse."

"Maybe I did."

Ines nodded as if that was the answer she trusted most. "Good. Then you understand the shape of it."

She had already done more than I had expected. She had pulled the key path, mapped the municipal sync window, and written the release note that would accompany the leak in language plain enough that panic might at least have a chance of becoming instruction.

If you are experiencing sensory overlap, confusion, memory discontinuity, or involuntary dream imagery, do not remain standing or operate machinery. The attached update suspends Vigil receptor maintenance and initiates sleep restoration. Seek a safe enclosed place.

It was the closest thing our civilization had to an evacuation order for reality.

The access door opened behind us.

Neither of us moved. There are moments when fear becomes so exact it behaves like concentration.

Vera stepped into the room with no security detail, no visible alarm, just a slate-gray coat over her work clothes and the expression she wore when a discussion had become tedious.

"Ines," she said. "Leave."

"No," Ines said.

Vera looked at me instead. "I am trying to prevent you from turning a contained medical problem into a civilizational event."

"It's not contained."

"It was containable."

She shut the door behind her. Somewhere above us pumps moved distribution stock through the building with a low arterial hum. It sounded obscenely alive.

"Do you know what happened when Vigil launched?" Vera asked. "Domestic accident rates fell. Long-haul fatalities fell. Emergency waiting lists halved. Parents got back a third of their working life. Entire regions came out of economic stagnation because time itself stopped being scarce in the old way. You think history will remember a few thousand drift events against that?"

"It won't stop at a few thousand."

"Because people like you escalate signal into catastrophe." For the first time her voice sharpened. "Bodies are conservative. Society cannot be."

I thought of Dalia gripping her untouched cup. The magistrate in Lyon forgetting her children. My own hand raised to knock on a door that had not existed for twenty years because some buried part of my mind had smelled jasmine and found a sleeping person inside the impossible.

"You call it residue because that's easier than calling it debt," I said. "We did not remove sleep. We repossessed it and left the brain to collect in fragments."

Vera's gaze moved to the locked queue on my tablet. "And your answer is to drop millions of people where they stand?"

"My answer is to stop pretending wakefulness is real just because it's profitable."

She held my eyes for a long moment. When she spoke again, the anger was gone, which made her more dangerous.

"Martin, if you release that protocol tonight, planes divert, trains halt, surgeries stall, security systems fail, markets suspend, and children watch adults fall unconscious in supermarkets and on stairwells and behind the wheel. There will be injuries. There may be deaths. If you wait, we can stage a managed transition."

"You had eighteen months."

That landed. Not dramatically. Just enough.

Ines had edged one hand toward the console without drawing Vera's attention. The sync countdown ticked in the corner of my screen: 00:01:12.

Vera saw it then and moved fast. Faster than I would have thought. She crossed the room and grabbed the tablet from my hand.

Ines hit the console.

For one terrible second nothing happened. Then the maintenance lights went out, the emergency strip at floor level came on red, and every display in the room flashed into manual recovery mode. Vera cursed under her breath. I had never heard her do that before. She was still holding the tablet, but the locked queue had already detached from local control and jumped to the sync buffer.

"What did you do?" she snapped.

"Bought us fourteen seconds," Ines said.

Vera lunged for the emergency kill panel.

I caught her wrist. She was stronger than I expected, all tendon and command. We hit the wall hard enough to jar my teeth. The tablet slid across the floor, screen bright in the red light. She drove an elbow into my chest. I lost air, kept hold, and felt the absurd intimacy of struggling with the woman who had taught me how to write risk summaries without legal exposure.

Above us a chime sounded through the building.

Municipal synchronization window open.

Vera froze. Not because she was afraid. Because she understood systems. She knew the sound meant the update had already gone wider than the room.

The emergency screens came alive one by one, not with Nexora branding but with the plain black-and-white release packet Ines had prepared. For a heartbeat the text stood there in silence. Then every connected device in the room, and likely in half the city, emitted the same soft confirmation tone.

Update received.

Vera closed her eyes.

It was the first time in all the years I had known her that she looked her age.

None of us spoke. There was nothing useful left to argue.

The first visible effect came seven minutes later.

We climbed to street level because staying underground felt like cowardice. Gran Via was still lit, but no longer with its old confidence. People had stopped moving at one shared velocity. Some sat down abruptly against shopfronts or on the curb, blinking as if light itself had become heavy. A man in a courier vest lay on his side beneath a pharmacy screen and slept with his mouth open while packages remained strapped to his back. A woman leaned against a bus shelter, smiled at no one, and slid carefully to the pavement as though obeying a forgotten instruction. Above them, luxury towers kept projecting silent adverts into mist, but the images were already tearing where the building systems failed to refresh them.

Cars did not crash in the cinematic way I had feared. Most of them simply stopped. Smart traffic took over where it could. Elsewhere, engines idled while drivers bowed their heads onto their own arms. On balconies, in offices, across the glass faces of towers, lights began to go out in irregular handfuls. Not a blackout. A surrender.

Emergency sirens multiplied, then thinned as the responders themselves succumbed in waves and backup protocols took over. Somewhere distant I heard laughter, hysterical and relieved. Somewhere else I heard sobbing. The city had no shared script for what was happening. It only had bodies, and the bodies had remembered before the law did.

My own limbs had started to ache with a depth I had never known. Not pain exactly. Weight. As if years had rushed back into muscle and bone all at once, billing me for every borrowed hour.

Ines touched my sleeve. "You need to get inside before it hits fully."

"What about you?"

"I have three blocks." She gave me a look almost affectionate in its impatience. "Go home, Martin. Try not to be morally symbolic on the pavement."

Vera had remained a few steps away, watching the avenue change species around her. She did not look at me when she spoke.

"If this works," she said, "they'll call you a terrorist for a year and a savior after that. Or the reverse."

"If it works," I said, "they'll have to sleep first."

I left them there and walked east through a city that was becoming strange by becoming human again. Doorways were full of people lying under coats improvised into blankets. Offices had gone dim floor by floor. Branded wayfinding ribbons flickered and died across storefront glass. A bakery that had never closed in four years stood dark except for one back lamp burning over trays of cooling bread nobody would sell until morning.

At my building I climbed six flights because the lift had stalled between levels. My apartment had always felt temporary, designed for a life that only paused to shower and change clothes. I stood in the middle of it and saw, for the first time, how little room I had left for stillness.

Outside, the usual urban hum was collapsing into pockets. A siren. A far-off shout. Then less, and less.

I set my tablet on the counter and watched new alerts flood in: unauthorized biomedical release, municipal safety guidance, market suspension notices, legal injunctions already overtaken by biology. Under them all ran the same incoming feed from ordinary users:

I can smell my grandmother's house.

My son is asleep on the sofa. Is that normal.

I dreamed before I even reached the bed.

What do I do with eight dark hours.

I found an old blanket in storage and do not know why I am crying.

I went to the cupboard where I kept cleaning supplies and took out the folded spare duvet I had not used since launch. It smelled faintly of cardboard and lavender detergent. When I spread it over the bed, the gesture felt ceremonial and embarrassingly intimate, as if I were preparing a body. Maybe I was. The body that had waited underneath the schedules and dosage reminders and confidence metrics. The body that had not stopped asking for night just because we had made night unfashionable.

I lay down fully dressed. Through the window I could see only three towers still lit in any serious way. The rest of the city had gone soft. Not dead. Not defeated. Suspended in the oldest rhythm we had ever had.

For years we had called sleep a defect, a waste, a private failure of endurance. We had built an economy on the insult. Now the insult was over. The bill had arrived.

My eyes closed before I meant them to.

In the last second before I crossed over, I felt no corridor, no false kitchen, no dream intruding where it did not belong. Only the immense, almost unbearable relief of something finally happening in its proper place.
