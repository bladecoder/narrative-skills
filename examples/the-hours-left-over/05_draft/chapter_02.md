# Chapter 2

I did not file Dalia before the deadline. Vera sent two messages, then one marked urgent, then came down to my station herself.

She had the kind of still face that made everyone around her perform order on instinct. Nothing in her expression suggested fatigue because nobody in our world was allowed to look as if they had been worn down by time.

"If this is caution," she said, "I admire it. If this is romanticism, I don't."

"The cluster isn't clean."

"No cluster is clean. That's why we have jobs."

I turned my monitor toward her. Dalia's transcript. The Lyon magistrate. The Seville scheduler. My unauthorized archive note, minimized but not gone.

Vera read quickly, one hand resting on the back of my chair. "These are still drift events."

"One of them nearly drove a freight carrier into a retaining wall."

"And did not. Because the system worked."

I wanted to tell her about the metro, about my mother's impossible apartment door, about the word asleep striking me like a physical blow. I said none of it. I had not yet decided whether silence was caution or cowardice.

"I want full archive access," I said.

Vera straightened. "Denied."

"On what basis?"

"On the basis that archived noise invites pattern delusion in analysts who have not reset recently."

Reset. Another polite word. She tapped the edge of my terminal. "Disposition the case as moderate drift. Recommend dosage review and hydration. Then go home for two hours."

"Nobody goes home for two hours."

"Then go somewhere with less glass," she said, and walked away.

I sent Ines Baeza a one-line message through an internal channel nobody monitored carefully because it had been built for dashboard maintenance:

`Need historical intrusion comparators. Coffee?`

She answered three minutes later.

`Only if by coffee you mean treason.`

We met at a public cafeteria under Plaza de Espana, one of the enormous sleepless halls where breakfast food and surgical scrubs and legal consultations were available at every hour. Clinics rented booths along the far wall. A pawn window glowed under a ramen sign. Half the tables had charging docks embedded in them for cuffs, tablets, and licensed neural accessories. The city had once used cafes to interrupt the day. Now they existed to prevent interruption.

Ines arrived carrying two paper cups and the expression of someone who had already decided the news was bad and was annoyed to have been right.

"You look awful," she said.

"Thank you."

"I mean clinically interesting."

I told her about Dalia. Not about my own intrusion. Then I showed her the archive note.

Ines read it without blinking. "You know they scrubbed most of Phase III from the live training repository."

"I know."

"You know that means somebody above Vera."

"Also yes."

She slid her cup toward me. "Good. Then I don't have to waste time warning you this is not a rogue manager softening a bad week. This is architecture."

Ines worked signal integrity, which meant she spent her days proving whether a pattern was real enough to deserve executive attention. She had an almost religious contempt for bad statistics and a practical loyalty to no one. There was an old cracked implant port behind her left ear that she never covered with makeup. If she was willing to say architecture, it meant she had already seen the shape of the lie.

"Can you get me the trial set?" I asked.

"Can? Yes. Should? No." She glanced up as servers moved behind the counter with the frictionless speed common to people who had not slept since adolescence. "There are ninety-one intrusion-compatible reports in the last twelve days. Public figure users are overrepresented. High cumulative exposure. Latest band update in eighty-six cases."

My mouth went dry. "Ninety-one?"

"That's just what survives the language filters." She lowered her voice. "There are more under transport error, emotional derealization, and workplace disorientation. Nobody wants the model to see a category if the category has legal consequences."

The lights above the pastry case blurred.

I looked down, expecting dizziness, and saw instead a white tablecloth under my hands. Porcelain. A bowl of green plums. Sun on a kitchen tile floor.

My mother sat across from me with her cheek resting in her palm. She was not dead in the vision because she had not yet died in that kitchen. She was only tired, the old human kind of tired that had once carried tenderness inside it. Her eyes were closed. Her breathing lifted one shoulder and let it fall.

"Martin."

Ines's voice came from far away. I tried to answer and heard a spoon ring against porcelain.

"Martin."

The tablecloth vanished. The cafeteria came back in a hard white snap. My cup had tipped over. Coffee spread across the table and onto my wrist. Ines had one hand around my forearm so tightly it hurt.

"How long?" I asked.

"Nine seconds." She studied me. "Do not lie to me now."

I wiped coffee from my cuff with the useless precision of panic. "The first one was fifty-seven."

Ines sat back. "And there it is."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Around us the cafeteria continued its smooth public hum. Trays slid. Screens updated. Cutlery touched ceramic. A sleepless civilization carried on with the confidence of something that did not know its own center was failing.

Ines opened her tablet and rotated it toward me.

"I pulled what I could before permissions narrowed," she said. "Animal trials first. Sleep architecture markers suppressed behaviorally, not neurologically. The mice kept moving while sections of their brains entered REM-pattern bursts. Then the human addenda. They called it segmented dream discharge."

Charts scrolled past: cortical activity spikes, emotional regulation metrics, memory fragmentation indices. Then a summary memo signed eighteen months before launch.

`Long-term cumulative exposure likely to produce mixed-state cognition. Subject may remain motor-active while dream-state imagery and associative logic enter waking sensorium.`

Below that, in a later hand:

`Commercial risk of disclosure exceeds current harm profile. Continue monitoring.`

I heard myself laugh, once, without amusement. "Current harm profile."

"Wait." Ines tapped again.

The next file was worse because it had numbers. Population adoption curves, exposure bands, convergence dates. A red projection window opened at the top of the graph.

`At present expansion rate, reality-dream convergence event becomes probable in metropolitan high-density zones within 18-24 months.`

"They're still pushing the booster next week," Ines said.

"What booster?"

"Adaptive update. New binding stabilizer. Public line is improved emotional smoothness and focus retention." Her mouth tightened. "Private line is market confidence."

Vera called while I was still reading. I almost let it ring out, then answered because defiance is cleaner after you know your ground.

"Where are you?" she asked.

"Off campus."

"Good. Stay there until you decide whether you're still employable."

"You knew."

There was a pause so small most people would not have heard it.

"I knew the same thing every adult in this industry knows," Vera said. "There is no intervention at scale without residue. Vigil gave the species usable time. We do not destabilize civilization because the body misses an obsolete rhythm."

"It isn't missing it," I said. "It's forcing it back in."

"In manageable fragments."

"You modeled convergence."

"We modeled scenarios. Martin, listen to me carefully. If confidence breaks, transport fails, hospitals fail, supply chains fail, half the labor law of Europe collapses in forty-eight hours. You are treating this as a moral puzzle because your sample size is human. Mine is social."

I looked at the graphs under my hand, at the red band crossing the city population curve like a wound.

"Then your sample size is wrong," I said, and ended the call.

Ines watched me in silence. After a moment she said, "If you leak this raw, they'll call it contaminated analysis from an unstable employee."

"They're not wrong about the unstable part."

"No," she said. "But they are wrong about the analysis."

She pushed another file across. This one was operational, not scientific. Distribution hub architecture. Certified update pathways. Municipal synchronization windows. I understood what she was showing me before she said it.

"The same system that pushes dose updates could push a reversal," I said.

"Not a cure. A restoration protocol. It would force people into actual sleep if enough receptors accepted it."

"At population scale?"

"Chaotically." She did not soften the word. "Ambulance demand would spike. Trains would halt. Offices would drop where they stood. Some people would resist the onset and injure themselves trying to stay vertical."

"And if nobody does it?"

Ines looked around the cafeteria, at the polished tile, the bright menu boards, the thousands of functioning strangers. "Then we wait until people start dreaming with both eyes open in operating theaters and control rooms."

The emergency feed on the wall changed. A transit advisory slid across the lower third in clean blue text: temporary service pause due to clustered orientation incidents on Lines 2 and 6. At the same time, half the room received the message through private overlays; I could tell by the synchronized refocusing of their eyes.

Clustered orientation incidents.

Somewhere in the city a train had filled with people who no longer fully agreed on where they were.

I closed the file and felt something inside me settle into place with the cold finality of a lock turning.

"Get me the update keys," I said.
