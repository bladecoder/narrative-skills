# Chapter 1

At 03:12 the city still looked like a machine that had forgotten how to power down. Tram lines hissed blue over wet pavement. Delivery drones crossed above Gran Via like metal swifts while ad fog climbed the glass towers in translucent layers of branded weather. Whole office floors burned white behind smart windows, and between them ran the thin traffic of maintenance bridges, couriers, and contract medics. People moved with the smooth, depthless energy Vigil had given everybody four years earlier. The old language remained out of habit. We still said dawn, midnight, graveyard shift. But those were decorative terms now, like fireplace or stable. Nobody in the city did anything so inefficient as surrender eight hours to darkness.

I worked in adverse-effect oversight for Nexora Therapeutics, which meant I spent my life translating damage into acceptable vocabulary. Hallucination became sensory drift. Collapse became transient motor pause. Panic became adjustment distress. The system rewarded precision and punished drama, and I had gotten good enough at the job that I could calm myself with the shape of a report before I had read what it contained. The building itself helped. Soft glass. Responsive walls. Corridor lights that adjusted to your biometric calm score. Nexora liked to make even denial feel ergonomic.

That morning the queue held thirty-one flagged cases. Twenty-eight were the usual noise: users double-dosing to feel sharper, freelancers stacking unapproved stimulants on top of Vigil, one warehouse coordinator who had mistaken a migraine aura for a product event because the world no longer left room for ordinary suffering. The other three had been tagged by the pattern monitor under a new severity flag: intrusion-compatible phenomenology.

I hated the phrase on sight.

I opened the first file. Female, forty-two, municipal freight route supervisor, no prior psychiatric events, excellent adherence record. Subject reported "loss of roadway continuity" lasting forty-nine seconds, followed by inappropriate emotional response and recovered orientation. The transcript attached below had been redacted once already, which meant someone upstream had found it inconvenient.

I requested the full recording and took the descent elevator through three security skins to interview suite C.

Dalia Ventosa was waiting with both hands around a paper cup she was not drinking. She wore the orange reflective jacket of overnight freight, though overnight did not mean anything anymore except traffic density. Her eyes were bloodshot in the old way, the way people used to look after too little sleep. I noticed the detail and disliked myself for noticing it as evidence.

"Mr. Arce?" she said.

"Martin is fine."

"I already told the other one."

"Then tell me again."

That was not empathy. Repetition changed testimony. It showed you where memory hardened and where it leaked.

She let out one breath through her nose. "I was on the M-30 feeder with a clean load and full visibility. Then the road was gone."

"Gone how?"

"Not dark. Not blank. Gone." She watched my face to see whether I was going to file her under unstable. "I was in a corridor. Red carpet, white doors. Seven of them, maybe eight. There was music behind one door. Somebody was frying garlic. I knew I was late for something, only not for the route. For something older."

I kept my hands still on the table.

"How long did that last?"

"I don't know in there. Outside, they said forty-nine seconds." She swallowed. "When I came back I was three lanes over and halfway onto the emergency shoulder. The truck corrected because the guidance system caught it. If the system had been offline, I would have killed someone."

"Had you taken your scheduled dose?"

She laughed once, without humor. "You think I forgot the miracle drug that pays my mortgage?"

I let that pass. "What happened after orientation recovered?"

"I cried." She seemed ashamed of that more than of anything else. "Not because I was scared. Because I could still smell the garlic. Because for a second I knew that corridor better than the road I've driven every week for three years."

The recording had summarized this as inappropriate emotional response.

"Did the corridor feel familiar?" I asked.

"No. That's the worst part. It felt mine."

Something tightened under my ribs. I had read stranger statements and signed off on them with less thought than it took to drink a coffee. But Dalia spoke with the flat accuracy of people who had not decided whether they were allowed to be frightened yet.

"Any childhood sleep disorder?" I asked, because procedure demanded I ask.

"We don't do childhood sleep anymore," she said. "My son asked me last year what a bed was for before storage."

I almost smiled, but she was not joking.

When the interview ended, I stood in the observation corridor with the transcript open in my hand. Behind the one-way glass Dalia remained seated at the table, cup untouched, staring at the seam in the wall as if she expected it to open into another life.

My terminal vibrated against the smartglass rail. Vera Cid wanted the case disposition in under ten minutes.

I should have marked it moderate drift, environmental stress amplification, follow-up in thirty days. That was what the queue wanted from me. That was what the city wanted from all of us who worked for Nexora: not truth exactly, but continuity.

Instead I reopened the cluster view.

The other two cases had happened within six hours of Dalia's. A pediatric logistics scheduler in Seville who lost a minute in an elevator and reported hearing ocean surf inside the walls. A magistrate in Lyon who forgot the names of her children and then recalled a blue horse standing in her kitchen. All long-term users. All compliant. All recently pushed to the latest optimization band.

I requested archived comparators, expecting nothing, and got a permissions warning I had never seen before:

`Legacy trial material contains unstable narrative events. Escalate only if operationally necessary.`

Operationally necessary. Not medically. Not ethically. Operationally.

I sent Vera a holding note that bought me twenty minutes and left the building through the staff concourse. The concourse fed into the public metro under a banner the size of a chapel wall: VIGIL GAVE US THE HOURS WE DESERVED. The ad showed a woman barefoot in a sunlit kitchen at 02:00, smiling over a bowl of fruit as if she had personally defeated biology. Beneath it, a row of civic update kiosks pulsed green for users whose subscriptions were current.

The platform smelled of ozone and bakery fat. Trains arrived every ninety seconds because nothing in the city had to rest between cycles anymore. Above the tracks, retinal-safe guidance arrows crawled across a polymer ribbon, steering commuters by line color and productivity district. People stood with the clean alertness that had become more normal than drowsiness had ever been. Their faces were composed, productive, politely absent. A teenager in courier chrome blinked through a private overlay only he could see.

I boarded, found a place by the door, and opened Dalia's file again. I was reading the line about the corridor when the carriage lights fluttered.

It was a tiny thing. A half-second dip. Enough for any old grid. Enough for nobody else to care.

Then the handrail under my palm turned warm, almost fleshy, and I knew with total certainty that I was standing in the hall outside my mother's apartment.

The certainty was complete and insane. I smelled starch and the cheap jasmine soap she had used when I was a boy. I saw the brass number on the door, a dent in the lower panel where I had kicked a football into it at eleven, the thin line of yellow light underneath. Someone was sleeping inside. That fact came with the force of grief. Sleeping. Not absent, not dead, not optimized out of the species. Asleep.

When the train doors chimed, I was no longer on the carriage.

I was standing on the platform at Tribunal in front of apartment 4B, though there were no apartments there, only maintenance doors and a vending wall. My hand was raised as if I had been about to knock. Commuters went around me with clipped annoyance. A child in a school badge looked up at my face and pulled closer to his father. His lenses flashed as his guardian app tagged me as a possible disturbance.

My watch said I had lost fifty-seven seconds.

I lowered my hand slowly. On the dark glass of the vending wall my reflection looked ordinary, but there was sweat on my neck and an ache behind my eyes that felt older than the morning.

My work tablet had slipped half out of my bag. The archive request screen was still open, though I had no memory of touching it. One line sat expanded under the permissions warning, exposed as if I had tapped the wrong place while absent.

`Phase III note, suppressed before rollout: Vigil maintained wake compliance but did not resolve sleep pressure. Dream-state processing persisted under constraint.`

Below that, in a clipped hand I recognized from old internal training packets, someone had added a second line.

`Intrusions were not exceptions. They were remainder.`

I looked past my reflection and into the glass as if the platform itself might answer back. Behind me the city moved with its usual tireless speed. Somewhere above ground offices brightened toward a morning nobody needed. I had spent years helping to preserve that brightness. For the first time since I joined Nexora, it occurred to me that the hours we had been given were not free hours at all.

They were hours left over from something unpaid.
